What the video actually describes. The Fox News segment covers the Pentagon's FORTRESS plan, which requires major U.S. military bases to run on their own power, water, fuel and communications for at least 90 days if a major war damaged civilian infrastructure. It's a plan for military bases. It is not an order or warning for civilians to evacuate or stockpile. The post's caption saying the Pentagon and FBI are "calling on citizens" goes further than the report itself does.

Even so, preparing is smart. The same kit covers far likelier events: earthquakes, wildfires, multi-day power shutoffs, storms, and water main breaks. Build for those first. Then you're ready for worse.

Water

28gallons to store
47-gal containers

Plan on 1 gallon per person per day (FEMA guideline), plus roughly 0.5 gal/day per pet. Add more in hot weather or for anyone nursing or sick. The filter is your backup for everything beyond what you stored.

Bleach purifier calculator

8 drops

Use only plain, unscented bleach with no additives. Stir, then wait 30 minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine. If it doesn't, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. Run cloudy water through a cloth or coffee filter first.

Methods, best to worst

Boiling (most reliable)
Bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft). Let it cool covered. To improve the flat taste, pour it back and forth between two clean containers.
Sawyer-type filter
Removes bacteria and protozoa but not viruses or chemicals. For questionable water, filter first and then add bleach. Backflush after use and never let it freeze, because ice cracks the fibers invisibly and the filter stops working.
Hidden water already in your home
  • Water heater tank (30–50 gal). Turn off the power or gas first, open the drain valve at the bottom, and discard the first sediment.
  • Toilet tank (not the bowl). Fine to drink after treatment, as long as you don't use tank cleaners.
  • Pipes: open the highest faucet, then drain from the lowest one.
  • Fill the bathtub bladder (WaterBOB) the moment an outage or boil-water alert starts.
Never drink
Water from pools or hot tubs (except for flushing), radiators, waterbeds, or floodwater. Avoid any water that may hold fuel or chemicals, because filters and bleach don't remove those.

When the power goes out

  1. Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge keeps food safe about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24 (USDA).
  2. Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the pantry.
  3. If perishables sit above 40°F for more than 2 hours, throw them out. When in doubt, throw it out.
  4. Cook outside only. Camp stoves, grills and generators make carbon monoxide.

Medical

Gear doesn't save lives. Trained hands do. Take a free Stop the Bleed class (stopthebleed.org) and a Red Cross CPR/First Aid course. Do this before you buy anything fancy.

Life threats, in order

1 · Severe bleeding
  1. Call 911 if phones work. Put on gloves if you have them.
  2. Arm or leg, spurting or pooling: apply a tourniquet 2–3 inches above the wound, never over a joint. Tighten the windlass until the bleeding stops, then lock it. Write the time on it. It will hurt, and that's normal.
  3. Torso, neck or groin: pack the wound tightly with hemostatic or plain gauze, then press hard with both hands for at least 3 minutes (10 for plain gauze).
  4. Wrap with a pressure bandage. Don't remove a tourniquet. That's for medical professionals to do.
2 · Not breathing / no pulse (hands-only CPR)
  1. Call 911 and have someone find an AED.
  2. Push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about 2 inches deep at 100–120 per minute (the beat of "Stayin' Alive").
  3. Don't stop until help or an AED arrives, or you can't physically continue.
3 · Choking
If the person can't cough or speak, give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades, then 5 abdominal thrusts. Keep alternating. If they become unresponsive, start CPR.
Burns
Cool the burn under clean running water for 20 minutes, not ice. Remove rings and watches. Cover loosely with plastic wrap or a clean non-stick dressing. Get help for burns bigger than the person's palm, or on the face, hands or genitals.
Dehydration / diarrhea
Use electrolyte packets. If you have none, use the WHO home recipe: 1 liter safe water + 6 level tsp sugar + ½ level tsp salt. Sip often. Get help urgently for confusion, no urine for 8+ hours, or bloody stool.
Hypothermia (no heat in winter)
Signs are shivering, clumsiness, and slurred speech. Get the person out of wet clothes, wrap them in blankets with an emergency blanket outside, and give warm sweet drinks if they're fully alert. Warm the trunk first, not the arms and legs. Sleep in one room, and keep everyone together.
Heat illness
Cramps or heavy sweating: rest in shade and drink fluids. Hot skin plus confusion means heat stroke, which is an emergency. Cool them aggressively with wet cloths and fanning, or ice packs on the neck, armpits and groin.

Grid-down playbook

First hour

First 72 hours

Carbon monoxide kills more people after disasters than most hazards. Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or propane heater indoors or in a garage, even with the door open. Keep generators at least 20 ft from windows. Keep the battery CO alarm on.

Phone & power

  • Turn on Low Power Mode, lower screen brightness, and turn off Bluetooth and background refresh.
  • Text instead of calling. Texts often get through when calls fail.
  • Save offline maps of your area in advance, and save this page on your phone.
  • Charge the power bank from the solar panel during the day, then charge phones at night.
  • Use the NOAA weather radio for official information when cell and internet service are down.

Sanitation (people forget this)

  • If water pressure is gone, don't flush. Line a bucket with two heavy bags and add a scoop of kitty litter after each use.
  • Tie off the bags and keep them sealed outside, away from water sources.
  • Wash hands or use sanitizer every time. Diarrhea spreads fast in outages.

Should you get a firearm?

This decision is yours. Here are the facts that should go into it.

1 · It doesn't fit this budget

Reliable handgun or shotgun$350–650
Lockbox or safe (required for safe storage)$50–200
Training class$100–250
Practice and defensive ammo$60–150
Realistic total$560–1,250

That's most or all of your $1,000, and it would leave you with no water, food or medical supplies. In nearly every real disaster, those are what keep people alive. If you want one, buy it with separate money after this kit is done.

2 · The honest tradeoffs

Reasons people choose to
  • Self-defense when police response could be slow or absent
  • Deterring a break-in
  • Hunting for food (shotgun or rifle) in rural areas
Risks to weigh
  • Large studies link a gun in the home to higher risk of suicide and accidental shootings among household members
  • Theft: unsecured guns are commonly stolen
  • Without regular training, a gun can be a liability under stress

Questions to ask yourself: Will you train and practice regularly? Can you store it locked every time? Are there kids, visitors, or anyone in the home who is going through a hard time?

3 · If you live in California

  • You must pass the Firearm Safety Certificate (FSC) written test, which most dealers can give the same day.
  • There is a 10-day waiting period for every gun. You can't buy one in a hurry during an emergency, so decide early if you're going to.
  • You must be 21+ for any purchase from a dealer, and you're limited to one gun per 30 days.
  • For handguns, you'll do a safe-handling demonstration at pickup, and you need an approved lock or safe.

Other states differ a lot. Check your state attorney general's website. This isn't legal advice.

4 · Cheaper security that works without a gun

  • Door reinforcement: 3" screws in the strike plate and hinges, plus a door security bar (~$30)
  • Solar motion lights outside (~$25)
  • Know your neighbors. A street that looks out for each other is the strongest security there is.
  • Keep your preps quiet so people don't know what you have
  • Pepper spray (legal in most states for adults; check limits)
